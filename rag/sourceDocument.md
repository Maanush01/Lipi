# Akka Mahadevi and the Vachana Sahitya Movement

## Vachana Sahitya — Overview

Vachana sahitya is a form of rhythmic writing in Kannada that evolved in the
11th century and flourished in the 12th century, as part of the Sharana
movement. The word "vachana" literally means "(that which is) said." These
are direct, simply-worded prose-poems, notable for their simplicity and
directness, often addressing social issues and personal devotion.

They were composed in Kannada by saints of the Lingayat faith, most notably
Basavanna, Akka Mahadevi, and Allama Prabhu. Vachanas critique rituals and
caste discrimination, advocating a form of worship centered on Shiva,
envisioned as a universal god.

More than 200 Vachana writers (Vachanakaras) have been recorded, and more
than thirty of them were women. About 800 sharanas practiced this form of
God-realization and recorded their experiences.

The socio-political center of the movement was Kalyana (present-day
Basava Kalyana, in Bidar district), where the "Anubhava Mantapa" — an
academy/assembly hall for spiritual and philosophical debate — was
established.

## Akka Mahadevi — Life

Akka Mahadevi was a 12th-century philosopher, poet, and mystic (c. 1130–1160),
and a prominent member of the Lingayatism denomination of Hinduism.

- **Born:** in Udutadi, near Shivamogga, in present-day Karnataka, around 1130.
- **Parents:** Some scholars suggest she was born to a couple named
  Nirmalshetti and Sumati (also given elsewhere as Nirmal Shetti and Sumati
  Shetty), both devotees of Shiva.
- **Died:** c. 1160, at Srisailam.

According to tradition, a local Jain king named Kaushika sought to marry
her. She is said to have laid down three conditions for the marriage,
including control over her time for devotion and scholarly conversation
rather than being required to spend it with the king. Accounts differ on
whether the marriage occurred: the medieval poet Harihara's biography
suggests the marriage was purely nominal, while other accounts (from
Camasara) suggest the conditions were rejected and the marriage never took
place.

Harihara's account says that when King Kaushika violated the conditions,
Akka Mahadevi left the palace, renouncing all possessions — including
clothes — to travel to Srisailam, the home of Shiva. Alternative accounts
suggest her renunciation was a response to the king's threats after she
refused him.

On her journey, she is believed to have passed through Basavakalyana
(Bidar district), where she met Allama Prabhu and Basavanna, two other
central figures of the Lingayat movement.

## The Honorific "Akka"

The sharanas of the Anubhava Mantapa — especially Basavanna, Chenna
Basavanna, Kinnari Bommayya, Siddharama, Allama Prabhu, and Dasimayya —
greeted her with the word "Akka" ("elder sister"), in recognition of her
place in the spiritual discussions there. It is from this point onward
that she is remembered as "Akka Mahadevi."

Her non-conformist ways caused consternation in the conservative society
of the time; even her guru, Allama Prabhu, initially faced difficulty in
including her in the Anubhava Mantapa gatherings.

## Asceticism

Akka Mahadevi is said to have refused to wear any clothing, becoming known
as the "naked saint" — a common practice among male ascetics of the era,
but considered shocking for a woman. Legend holds that her body was
protected/covered by her own hair due to her devotion, and she said this
covering was not for her own modesty but to protect others from
temptation.

## Works

- She is credited with **430 vachanas** (per some sources) or **about 350**
  lyric poems (per other sources, based on tracing her "ankita," or
  signature name).
- She also wrote two short prose works: **Mantrogopya** and
  **Yogangatrividhi** (also spelled Yogangatrividh).
- Her **ankita** (the signature name she used to address her chosen deity)
  is **"Chennamallikarjuna,"** referring to Shiva. Scholar A. K. Ramanujan
  translated this as "Lord, white as jasmine." A more literal translation,
  per Tharu and Lalita, is "Mallika's beautiful Arjuna."
- Her poetry frequently uses the metaphor of illicit or adulterous love to
  describe her devotion to Chennamallikarjuna, and touches on abandonment,
  carnal love, and separation.
- A. K. Ramanujan first popularized her vachanas in English through his
  translation collection **"Speaking of Siva."**
- Scholar Tejaswini Niranjana critiqued Ramanujan's translations in her
  1992 book **"Siting Translation"** for rendering the vachanas into a
  modern, universalist style easily consumable by Western readers.
- Kannada translator Vanamala Vishwanatha has worked on a newer English
  translation, potentially for the Murty Classical Library.

## Life in Three Phases (as described in tradition)

1. **First phase:** She renounced worldly objects and attractions.
2. **Second phase:** She discarded all object-based rules and regulations.
3. **Third phase:** She journeyed toward Srisaila (Srisailam), home of the
   Chennamallikarjuna temple, a holy site for Shiva devotees since before
   the 12th century.

Her spiritual journey is said to have ended at **Kadali**, a dense forest
area of Srisailam, where she experienced "aikya" (union) with
Chennamallikarjuna.

## Legacy

- She is regarded by modern scholars as a significant figure in female
  emancipation and is considered a major female figure in both Kannada
  literature and Karnataka's history.
- Roads and a university in Karnataka have been named after her (a
  women's university was renamed in her honor, reported in 2016).
- A junction in Shivamogga was named after her (reported 2016).
- In 2010, a bas-relief dating to the 13th century, believed to depict
  Akka Mahadevi, was discovered near Hospet, Karnataka. (Note: one source
  also references a separate 13th-century bas-relief find near
  Chitradurga in 2010 — sources vary slightly on the exact location.)

## Key Related Figures

- **Basavanna** — founder-associated figure of the Lingayat/Sharana
  movement; formulated much of its social philosophy; held open
  gatherings rejecting caste hierarchy.
- **Allama Prabhu** — provided the philosophical and mystical core of the
  movement; was Akka Mahadevi's guru figure at the Anubhava Mantapa.
- Other notable Vachana writers: Chennabasavanna, Siddharama, Kinnari
  Bommayya, Dasimayya, Hadapada Appanna, Madivala Machayya.
